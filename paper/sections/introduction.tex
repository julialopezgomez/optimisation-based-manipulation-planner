\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A robot opening a bottle, turning a valve or winding a crank cannot finish in
a single grasp: the wrist runs out of range long before the object reaches its
goal. It has to grasp, turn as far as it can, let go, rotate the wrist back
and grasp again. Planning this means deciding where to grasp and release, how
far to turn each time, how many times to regrasp, and how to move in between.
None of these can be decided on its own. An obstacle beside the object
shortens the usable part of the wrist range, which changes how far one grasp
can turn the object, and with it the number of grasps the task needs.

What makes this hard is how thin the feasible sets are, and the fact that the
task accumulates. For an arm and a hand, the configurations that
hold an object at a fixed grasp satisfy several equality constraints on the
pose of the hand, and so form a thin, curved set in a configuration space of
ten dimensions or more. Only its collision-free part is usable, and that part
lies precisely where the hand is closest to the obstacles. The object, meanwhile, moves only while held and keeps its angle once
released, so what the robot can still reach depends on all it has done so
far.

For a robot moving alone, convex decompositions of the collision-free
space~\cite{deits2015computing,dai2024certified,petersen2023growing,werner2024approximating}
made this kind of reasoning tractable: Graphs of Convex Sets return
trajectories that are globally optimal relative to the
decomposition~\cite{marcucci2023motion}. None of that machinery decides when
to grasp or release. Planners that do reason about regrasping either sample
the manipulation
graph~\cite{simeon2004manipulation,hauser2010multimodal,mirabel2016hpp},
enumerate a finite set of grasps~\cite{wan2017regrasp,lertkultanon2018certified},
or fix the sequence of grasps and optimise the
motions~\cite{toussaint2015lgp,levit2025regrasp}. Mixed-integer formulations
over convex sets, our own included~\cite{lopezgomez2025minf}, are complete and
optimal relative to the decomposition, but only for a number of grasps fixed
beforehand, and the indicator constraints that select among the sets are
poorly conditioned when those sets are thin
(Section~\ref{sec:method:miqp}). After a finite computation, none of them can
say that a plan uses the fewest grasps, or that no plan exists.

Footstep planning met the same obstacle and got past it by searching over sets
instead of points: CASSR~\cite{wang2026cassr} runs A* on convex sets of
footholds reachable from the start, planning 30 steps in under 125\,ms.
Regrasping asks for more than a change of domain: footholds come ready-made
from the environment, whereas grasping configurations must be built from
nonlinear kinematics, and a grasp, unlike a footstep, moves the object, so
reachability has to carry the object's state.

We build the manipulation graph of~\cite{simeon2004manipulation} out of convex
sets. A convex decomposition of free space gives the \emph{free sets}; a
convex inner approximation of the grasping configurations, grown from a seed
grasp along the null space of the grasp constraints~\cite{toussaint2024nlp},
gives the \emph{grasp set}; their intersections are the \emph{transition
sets}, the only places where the robot can grasp, turn and release the object.
We search them with an A* whose nodes pair a set with the interval of object
configurations reachable inside it. Convexity makes every point of a set
reachable from any other along a line that never leaves it, and the interval
makes propagation and pruning exact, so the search needs no sampling, no
integer variables and no horizon fixed in advance. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{transition sets}, a convex representation of the manipulation
    graph, built for a 7-DOF arm and validated against a collision checker
    where the decomposition is least reliable
    (Sections~\ref{sec:problem},~\ref{sec:method:free}--\ref{sec:method:graph});
  \item a \emph{reachability A*} over them, complete and returning the fewest
    grasps relative to the sets, with a solver-free stage that recovers the
    motions (Sections~\ref{sec:method:search}--\ref{sec:method:properties});
  \item an evaluation on cap-turning tasks, up to the 10-dimensional space of
    a Franka arm, hand and cap, against our mixed-integer planner and a
    sampling-based one (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:results}).

\end{itemize}
