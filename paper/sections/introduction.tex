\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Many manipulation tasks cannot be completed with a single grasp. Turning a
bottle cap with a wrist of limited range, cranking a valve, or reorienting an
object the hand cannot rotate far enough all force the robot to grasp, move,
release, reposition and grasp again. How many regrasps are needed, and where,
depends on the goal, on the robot's kinematics and on the obstacles around the
object. In our 10-DOF task, turning a cap by 4\,rad with a wrist that sweeps
2\,rad takes at least two grasps in free space. Obstacles that block part of
the sweep raise this to five (Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}). The
question to answer is one of \emph{reachability}: which object configurations
can be reached with $k$ grasps, and from which robot configurations each grasp
can start and end.

Footstep planning has the same structure and has made it explicit. A footstep
plan is a sequence of contacts of unknown length, each constrained by where
the previous ones allow the next foot to land. Planners moved from A* over
discretised footholds~\cite{chestnutt2005footstep,griffin2019footstep} to
mixed-integer programs over convex terrain
decompositions~\cite{deits2014footstep,aceituno2018simultaneous}, made fast
by $\ell_1$ relaxations~\cite{tonneau2020sl1m,song2021solving}, and to
planners built on explicit reachability
conditions~\cite{tonneau2018efficient}. Most recently,
CASSR~\cite{wang2026cassr} runs A* over nodes that are convex sets of
footholds reachable from the start. It plans 30 footsteps in under 125\,ms,
outperforming discretised A* by up to two orders of magnitude, and a
commercial mixed-integer solver. In
footstep planning, \emph{the number of contacts is an output of a reachability
computation over convex sets}.

Prehensile manipulation shares this combinatorial core, yet long regrasp
sequences have received much less attention. Sim\'eon
et al.~\cite{simeon2004manipulation} formalised manipulation planning in the
composite configuration space of robot and object. Paths alternate
\emph{transit}, where the robot moves alone, and \emph{transfer}, where it
holds the object, and switch where grasp and placement sets intersect. They
explored the resulting manipulation graph with probabilistic roadmaps. Since
then, regrasp sequences have mostly been planned over finite sets of grasps and
placements~\cite{tournassoud1987regrasping,alami1990geometrical,wan2017regrasp}.
With continuous grasp sets, planners sample, which gives at best probabilistic
completeness and is slow in the thin sets where grasps
happen~\cite{simeon2004manipulation,hauser2010multimodal,mirabel2016hpp}, or
optimise over a skeleton whose number of mode switches is guessed or
enumerated~\cite{toussaint2015lgp,levit2025regrasp}. To our knowledge, no
manipulation planner offers what CASSR offers for footsteps: a search whose
cost is the number of contact switches, whose nodes are continuous reachable
sets, and which is complete and optimal relative to its model of free space.

Learning does not close this gap, and multi-regrasp tasks show why. Learned
policies have reached impressive dexterity, from in-hand
reorientation~\cite{openai2020learning} to twisting lids off with two
multi-fingered hands~\cite{lin2024twisting}, and imitation learning covers
many everyday tasks~\cite{chi2023diffusion}. But the minimum number of
regrasps is a piecewise-constant function of the goal and the scene, roughly
$\lceil D/L\rceil$ for a turning angle $D$ and a longest collision-free wrist
sweep $L$. A policy trained on goals that need $k$ regrasps must extrapolate
to $k+1$, a longer horizon than any it was trained on, where errors
compound~\cite{ross2011reduction}. Moving an obstacle slightly can change $L$
and with it the discrete structure of the solution, not only its geometry. A
policy also cannot certify that a goal is unreachable with fewer grasps. A
planner that computes reachability on an explicit model handles these cases by
construction: a new goal is a new query, and a new obstacle layout is a new
model. \tocheck{an argument, not an experiment; keep claims at this level
unless a learned baseline is added.}

We bring the reachability view of footstep planning to multi-regrasp
manipulation. We build on convex decompositions of the collision-free
configuration space~\cite{werner2024approximating,werner2024faster}, the
representation behind globally optimal motion planning with Graphs of Convex
Sets~\cite{marcucci2023motion}. We intersect them with polytopes of grasping
configurations. The resulting \emph{grasp pieces} are the only sets in which
the object moves, the switch sets of the manipulation graph. Our A* pairs
each polytope with the set of object configurations reachable inside it. When
one object coordinate is coupled to one robot joint, this set is an interval,
propagation is interval arithmetic and dominance is exact. We arrived at this
design after a mixed-integer formulation~\cite{lopezgomez2025minf}, which
solved low-dimensional tasks, became numerically unreliable on a 7-DOF arm.
Its grasp polytopes are $10^{-3}$\,rad thick, nine orders of magnitude below
its big-M constant. \tocheck{anonymise the self-citation if double-blind.}
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a formulation of multi-regrasp planning as reachability over a convex
    decomposition, with grasp pieces as the switch sets between transit and
    transfer (Sections~\ref{sec:problem},~\ref{sec:method:graph});
  \item a reachability A* with an LP-only graph, an admissible and consistent
    heuristic and exact dominance. It returns a plan with the minimum number
    of grasp actions relative to the decomposition, of a length not fixed in
    advance, and a solver-free stage turns that plan into certified straight
    motions (Sections~\ref{sec:method:search}--\ref{sec:method:properties});
  \item grasp polytopes for a 7-DOF arm, built from a linearisation at an NLP
    sampling seed~\cite{toussaint2024nlp} that uses the exact invariance of
    the grasp to wrist rotation and a trust region
    (Section~\ref{sec:method:grasp});
  \item an evaluation up to the 10-DOF space of a Franka arm, hand and cap,
    with an analysis of the MIQP's failure. It shows that probabilistically
    certified decompositions concentrate their errors on grasp sets
    (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:discussion}).

\end{itemize}
