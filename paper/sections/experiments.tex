\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

Our experiments ask four questions. Does the search return plans with the
minimum number of grasps its sets allow, for any goal, from a single offline
stage? How does it compare with GCS on the same sets and with a sampling-based
manipulation planner, counting both offline and online cost? How does it scale
with the number of objects? And why must the decomposition be checked where
the transition sets lie?

\subsection{Scenes and implementation}
A Franka Panda~\cite{haddadin2022franka} turns caps about fixed vertical axes.
Its wrist is restricted to $[-1,1]$\,rad, so any turn larger than the wrist
range left free by the scene needs several grasps. Each scene has a floor just
under the caps and the robot base.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textsf{1cap\_2obs}} (10-DOF): seven arm joints, two fingers
    and one cap, 0.5\,m in front of the base, with two cylindrical obstacles
    (radius 5\,mm) beside it. The obstacles block part of the wrist range at
    the grasp.
  \item \textbf{\textsf{8caps\_3obs}} (17-DOF): eight caps on an arc of radius
    0.5\,m around the base, from $-120^\circ$ to $120^\circ$, and three radial
    walls (0.35 and 0.5\,m high) between groups of caps, so that moving between
    groups means going over or around a wall.
\end{itemize}
The comparison with the MIQP also uses a 3-DOF task (wrist, finger and cap of
a locked arm, 15 regions, a $2\pi$ turn). \tocheck{3-DOF task from the MInf
report (WSG gripper) or the locked Franka?}

\paragraph{Decomposition} Every scene is decomposed by one procedure. A cap's
collision geometry is a cylinder about its own joint axis, so no collision
depends on a cap angle: we compute $\CSfree$ in the 9-D space of arm and
fingers with the caps welded, which is exactly the slice of
Section~\ref{sec:method:free}. The procedure has four steps.
(1)~For each cap, a seed grasp is refined onto~\eqref{eq:grasp_hg} by an NLP.
(2)~IRIS-NP2~\cite{werner2024faster} grows regions along each grasp's
collision-free wrist runs, from an ellipsoid that is long along the wrist and
thin across it; a round initial ellipsoid gave regions only about 0.2\,rad
long in the wrist. (3)~A clique cover~\cite{werner2024approximating} with these
regions as initial sets runs to a 0.98 coverage target. (4)~Points that a
region calls free but the collision checker rejects are cut off by separating
planes, never cutting the free part of a grasp's wrist sweep: on dense wrist
sweeps at each grasp, around the caps and uniformly over the joint box.
Table~\ref{tab:offline} reports the result.

\paragraph{Grasp constraints} The constraints~\eqref{eq:grasp_hg} ask the
hand to be centred over the cap, within a height band, with its approach axis
$z_E={}^{C}R^{E}(x)e_z$ pointing down:
\begin{align}
  h(x) &= [\,p_x,\; p_y,\; z_{E,x},\; z_{E,y}\,]^\top, \label{eq:grasp_h}\\
  g(x) &= [\,p_z - z_{\max},\; z_{\min} - p_z,\; z_{E,z} + c\,]^\top, \label{eq:grasp_g}
\end{align}
with $p={}^{C}p^{E}(x)$, $[z_{\min},z_{\max}]=[0.105,0.110]$\,m and $c=0.9$,
and the fingers held within a 1\,mm band around the width of the cap. Here
$J_h$ has rank 4, leaving a six-dimensional null space. The grasp set
of~\eqref{eq:graspPoly} uses $\epsilon=10^{-3}$, $\rho=0.05$\,rad and
$\Lmin=0.05$\,rad. The wrist of the Panda satisfies the invariance of
Section~\ref{sec:method:grasp} exactly: sweeping it over its whole range at a
grasp leaves $h$ and $g$ unchanged to machine precision. With several caps,
each cap has its own grasp set, and a node carries one interval of $\alpha$ per
cap; a move inside a transition set of cap $k$ changes only the interval of
$\alpha_k$, and the heuristic~\eqref{eq:heuristic} becomes a sum over caps.

\paragraph{Implementation} The planner is in Python, with
Drake~\cite{tedrake2019drake} (v1.48). The graph needs tens of thousands of
small LPs (emptiness checks, Chebyshev centres, extreme points). We solve each
as the regularised QP $\min c^\top x+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\|x\|^2$ subject to
$Ax\le b$, with $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$, using the dual active-set method of
Goldfarb and Idnani~\cite{goldfarb1983dual}, which reports infeasibility, and
hence emptiness, directly. With MOSEK, every one of these solves checked a
licence out, which made the graph of \textsf{8caps\_3obs} take 15\,min.
MOSEK~\cite{mosek} solves the decomposition's programs, GCS and the second
stage, with one licence held throughout. All times are on one AMD Ryzen 9
9950X (16 cores, 32 threads, 60\,GB).

\subsection{Baselines and protocol}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol}

\paragraph{GCS on the same sets} The free sets $F_j$ and the transition sets
$T_k$, with $\alpha$ free within its limits, are the vertices, and the transit
and switch edges of Section~\ref{sec:method:graph} are the edges. Each visited
vertex holds one straight segment $r_0\to r_1$, with both ends in its set. The
coupling becomes linear constraints on these two points: $r_1^{\alpha}=r_0^{\alpha}$
in every $F_j$, and in every $T_k$
\[
r_1^{\alpha}-r_1^{w}=r_0^{\alpha}-r_0^{w}, \qquad r_1^{w}\ge r_0^{w},
\]
which is a forward stroke. A GCS path visits each vertex at most once, but a
plan can need several strokes in the same $T_k$, with returns in between. So
the graph is copied once per grasp:
\[
\text{approach}\to\text{grasp}_1\to\text{between}_1\to\cdots\to\text{grasp}_N\to\text{depart},
\]
with $N=\lceil D/\Lmax\rceil+1$ for a turn $D$. Every $\text{grasp}_n$ also
connects to the depart layer, and the approach layer also connects directly to
the goal. The cost is the path length, plus 10 per grasp, plus $\mu$ per other
change of set; ten exceeds any path length here, so the fewest grasps come
first, as in the search. The between layers hold the free sets that meet a
transition set; the approach and depart layers hold those, the free sets
containing $\xinit$ or $\xgoal$, and the free sets of the A*'s plan. We solve
it with Drake's \texttt{SolvePath}: a convex relaxation, rounded to at most 10
candidate paths, each finished by a convex restriction.

\paragraph{Manipulation RRT} As the sampling-based baseline we use the
Manipulation RRT of HPP~\cite{mirabel2016hpp}, which plans on the manipulation
graph~\cite{simeon2004manipulation}, built by HPP's constraint-graph factory on
the same robot and scene (exported from Drake, with Drake's collision
geometry and filters). The hand grasps the cap's handle 0.1075\,m above it,
pointing down, at any yaw; outside a grasp the cap joint is locked at its
current angle; and the fingers are locked at the grasp width. Motions are
validated continuously (HPP's \texttt{Dichotomy}, with a progressive projector
in constrained states): HPP's default discretised check returned plans that
Drake rejects. Paths are not shortcut.

\paragraph{Protocol} Every returned motion is re-checked with Drake: collisions,
the coupling (the cap moves only with the grasping wrist) and the goal. On
\textsf{1cap\_2obs}, every method shares one start and one goal, saved with the
sets, and the cap turns by $D\in\{0.5,1.5,2.4,3.2,6\}$\,rad. HPP runs 10 seeds
per query with a 60\,s limit. On \textsf{8caps\_3obs}, for each number $K$ of
caps to turn, we draw 20 random queries: $K$ random caps, each turned by a
random amount in $[0.5,\pi]$\,rad, summing to $2.5K$\,rad, with random start
and goal arm configurations in the free sets. The A* runs exactly and weighted
($w=1.5$), with a 60\,s limit. HPP runs 5 of those queries per $K$, 3 seeds
each, 60\,s each. Online times count each method's work per query: for the A*
the search and the second stage; for GCS building its layered graph and
solving it; for HPP its \texttt{solve()}. The offline stage is reported
separately (Table~\ref{tab:offline}), and is shared by the A* and GCS. Memory
is the peak extra resident memory; each full GCS solve runs in a fresh process.

\subsection{One cap: the A*, GCS and the Manipulation RRT}
\label{sec:results:gcs}
Table~\ref{tab:gcs} compares the three methods on \textsf{1cap\_2obs}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The A* always returns the fewest grasps}, $\lceil D/\Lmax\rceil$,
    in 0.010--0.037\,s, second stage included. It expands 112--398 nodes, in
    0.007--0.027\,s.
  \item \emph{Full GCS returns shorter paths, but not always the fewest
    grasps.} It is 40--55$\times$ slower (0.56--1.8\,s) and uses 0.8--1.4\,GB.
    At the two turns where it returns as many grasps as the A*, its path is 10
    and 19\% shorter. At the other three it returns one grasp more, which its
    own cost ranks lower: at $D=1.5$ the A*'s plan is a path of the same GCS
    graph with cost $16.03+2\cdot10=36.0$, against 44.0 for the plan returned.
    Rounding the relaxation carries no optimality guarantee, and here it missed
    the plan with the fewest grasps.
  \item \emph{GCS on the A* sequence} solves only the convex restriction of
    the same layered GCS graph to the A*'s sets, in 0.019--0.059\,s, A*
    search included. It returns the A*'s lengths to seven digits: the A*'s
    second stage (Section~\ref{sec:method:recovery}) is that same convex
    program, so it is optimal for the sets the A* chose. The length gap to
    full GCS at equal grasps therefore comes from the choice of sets, which
    the A* makes by the number of set changes, not by length.
  \item \emph{The Manipulation RRT solves only the one-grasp query}, in 7 of
    10 runs, in 0.04\,s (median), with paths 18\% longer than the A*'s. With
    two or more grasps it found no plan in any run, neither within 60\,s nor,
    on the scene without the floor, within 600\,s. Each regrasp needs the
    tree to reach a thin grasp set, turn within it and leave it, and a long
    chain of such steps is unlikely to be sampled.
\end{itemize}
Counting offline and online cost, the A* and GCS share the 2.8\,min
decomposition and the 0.09\,s graph; GCS then pays 0.6--1.8\,s and about
1\,GB per query, the A* at most 0.04\,s. The Manipulation RRT has no offline
stage, but does not solve the multi-grasp queries.

\paragraph{Where GCS spends its time} \texttt{SolvePath} does not solve the
mixed-integer program of GCS~\cite{marcucci2024shortest}: it solves its convex
relaxation and rounds it. The relaxation is one conic program in which every
edge carries a copy of the segment variables of both its ends, so its size
follows the number of edges, which grows with the number of grasps: from
29 vertices and 130 edges at $D=0.5$ to 77 and 346 at $D=6$, with time and
memory growing with them. The A* solves no program in its search: it does
interval arithmetic on extreme points computed offline. On an earlier
decomposition of the same scene, with 8 transition sets and 543 edges for one
grasp, the same GCS formulation took 44 and 93\,s for one and two grasps and
did not fit in 60\,GB at eight (Appendix~\ref{app:gcs}). The cost of GCS thus
depends on the number of transition sets, which grows with the number of
objects.

\paragraph{Why GCS needs the graph unrolled} In GCS a path is a sequence of
distinct vertices, and each vertex holds a single
segment~\cite{marcucci2024shortest}. A regrasp plan, however, comes back to
the same transition set once per grasp, and to the free sets where it lets go
and turns the wrist back. GCS can represent such a plan only after the graph is
copied by hand, with the coupling written on each copy and the number of
grasps bounded before solving: on a two-variable toy ($(w,\alpha)$ in a box, a
4\,rad turn, strokes of at most 2\,rad), a single copy of the grasp vertex is
infeasible, both relaxed and as the exact mixed-integer program. A node of the
reachability A* pairs a set with an interval of $\alpha$, so coming back to a
set with a different $\alpha$ is simply a new node, the number of grasps is an
output of the search, and the graph is built once for all queries.

\subsection{Eight caps: scaling with the number of objects}
\label{sec:results:scaling}
Table~\ref{tab:cap_scaling} reports \textsf{8caps\_3obs}. Of the 160
queries, 15 start or end outside the current free sets (they were drawn for
an earlier decomposition, and are kept so that HPP sees the same queries);
we leave them out.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Weighted A* solves every query}, 145 of 145, with time growing
    about linearly in $K$: from 0.13\,s for one cap to 1.6\,s for eight
    (15 grasps), all plans certified.
  \item \emph{Exact A* proves the minimum, but only for few caps.} It solves
    every query up to $K=3$, 15 of 20 at $K=4$ and none at $K=5$ within
    60\,s. With $K$ caps, a set can be reached with many combinations of cap
    intervals that do not dominate one another, and the heuristic, a sum of
    per-cap bounds, ignores the travel between caps.
  \item \emph{Weighting cost nothing here.} On all 69 queries both solved,
    the weighted search returned exactly as many actions as the exact one.
    Its bound guarantees at most $1.5$ times the minimum cost.
  \item \emph{The Manipulation RRT found no plan} in any of its 120 runs
    (5 queries and 3 seeds for each $K$), already at $K=1$, where a query
    turns one cap by 2.5\,rad and needs at least two grasps.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the decomposition must be checked at the grasps}
\label{sec:results:fidelity}
A planner whose guarantees are relative to its sets inherits their errors.
Region generators bound the fraction of a region's volume in collision, and
transition sets have almost no volume (Section~\ref{sec:method:graph}), so
that bound says nothing about them. On an earlier decomposition of
\textsf{1cap\_2obs}, generated by a clique cover alone, we swept the wrist
over $[-1,1]$ at the seed grasp, at 99 points. The collision checker found it
free only on $[-1,-0.19]$ and $[0.70,0.92]$\,rad, but all four decompositions
we had computed (IRIS-NP2 with 42 and 46 regions, two IRIS-ZO covers with 64)
called all 99 points free. A two-grasp plan for a 4\,rad turn was therefore
valid relative to the sets, but collided; with the true free interval of
0.81\,rad the minimum was five grasps, a different discrete structure, not
just a different geometry. This is why the decomposition seeds regions along
the free wrist runs at each grasp and cuts the false positives found on dense
wrist sweeps (steps 2 and 4). On the current decompositions those sweeps find
no false positive (Table~\ref{tab:offline}), and every plan of
Tables~\ref{tab:gcs}--\ref{tab:cap_scaling} passes the collision checker.
A dense check is not a certificate: certifying the thin transition sets with
C-IRIS~\cite{dai2024certified} would be.

\subsection{The trust region on the grasp set}
Without the trust region $\rho$, the extreme points that the graph
construction returns lie far outside the region where the linearisation holds:
on \textsf{1cap\_2obs} they lay 2.27\,rad from the seed in one arm joint, with
$\|h\|_\infty=0.95$ and the hand 0.80\,m away from the cap. With
$\rho=0.05$\,rad, $\|h\|_\infty$ stays near $2\times10^{-3}$ along the plans we
report \tocheck{numbers from a current run}.
