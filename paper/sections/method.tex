The planner has an offline stage, which depends only on the robot, the object
and the scene, and an online stage per query. Offline, it decomposes free
space into polytopes (Section~\ref{sec:method:decomp}), builds grasp polytopes
(Section~\ref{sec:method:grasp}), and intersects them into grasp pieces and a
polytope graph using only LPs (Section~\ref{sec:method:graph}). Online, a
reachability A* chooses the sequence of sets and grasp actions
(Section~\ref{sec:method:search}), and a placement stage turns it into
certified motions (Section~\ref{sec:method:placement}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:reach} illustrates both stages on our 10-DOF task.

\subsection{Convex decomposition of free space}
\label{sec:method:decomp}
We inner-approximate $\CSfree$ by polytopes
$P_j=\{q:A_jq\le b_j\}$, $j=1,\dots,J$, computed with clique covers of
visibility graphs~\cite{werner2024approximating} and IRIS-NP2
regions~\cite{werner2024faster} in Drake~\cite{tedrake2019drake}. Each region
is collision-free up to a fraction $\varepsilon$ of its volume, with
confidence $1-\delta$. Certified alternatives exist~\cite{dai2024certified}
but scale poorly to 10 dimensions. By Assumption~\ref{ass:task}(iii), the
object coordinate does not affect collisions. We therefore replace each region
by its slice at $\alpha=0$, $F_j=\{x:(x,0)\in P_j\}$, dropping rows that only
bound $\alpha$. This is sound: if $(x,0)$ is collision-free, so is $(x,\alpha)$
for every $\alpha$. The robot can then occupy any $x\in F_j$ at any object
angle, and the planner tracks $\alpha$ separately.

\subsection{Grasp polytopes for a 7-DOF arm}
\label{sec:method:grasp}
For a gripper with a few joints, $G$ is a box in configuration
space~\cite{lopezgomez2025minf}. For a 7-DOF arm, a grasp constrains the pose
of the hand frame $E$ relative to the cap frame $C$, a nonlinear function of
the arm joints. The hand must be centred above the cap, within a height band,
with its approach axis $z_E={}^{C}R^{E}(x)\,e_z$ pointing down:
\begin{align}
  h(x) &= [\,p_x,\; p_y,\; z_{E,x},\; z_{E,y}\,]^\top = 0, \label{eq:grasp_h}\\
  g(x) &= [\,p_z - z_{\max},\; z_{\min} - p_z,\; z_{E,z} + c\,]^\top \le 0, \label{eq:grasp_g}
\end{align}
with $p={}^{C}p^{E}(x)$, $[z_{\min},z_{\max}]=[0.105,0.110]$\,m and $c=0.9$.
The fingers are restricted to a 1\,mm band around the cap width. The set
$\{h=0,\,g\le0\}$ is a thin, curved manifold. We find a seed grasp $x_0$ on it
with the Gauss--Newton phase of NLP sampling~\cite{toussaint2024nlp} and
linearise around it:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G=\{x:\ & |h_0+J_h(x-x_0)|\le\epsilon,\ \ g_0+J_g(x-x_0)\le\epsilon,\\
        & x^{G}_{\min}\le x\le x^{G}_{\max},\ \ |x_j-x_{0,j}|\le\rho\ \ \forall j\in\mathcal{A}\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:graspPoly}
\end{equation}
where $h_0,J_h,g_0,J_g$ are evaluated at $x_0$, $\epsilon=10^{-3}$, and
$\mathcal{A}$ are the arm joints other than the wrist. Two properties of the
task shape this construction. First, the wrist axis passes through the origin
of $E$ along $z_E$, so rotating the wrist changes neither $p$ nor $z_E$: $h$
and $g$ are \emph{exactly} invariant along $w$. We verified this to machine
precision over the whole wrist range. The wrist columns of $J_h$ and $J_g$
therefore vanish, and $G$ spans the full wrist range. Second, $J_h$ has rank
4, so without the last constraint in~\eqref{eq:graspPoly} the null directions
of $J_h$ would be bounded only by the joint limits. The extreme points that
the graph construction computes would then lie where the linearisation is
meaningless. Without this \emph{trust region}, they moved an arm joint by
2.27\,rad from $x_0$, with $\|h\|_\infty=0.95$ and the hand 0.80\,m away from
the cap. With $\rho=0.05$\,rad, $\|h\|_\infty$ stays around $2\times10^{-3}$
along our plans \tocheck{guide numbers, q0 run}. Seeds drawn from other
components of the grasp manifold, e.g., with NLP sampling's manifold-RRT
interior phase, give further grasp polytopes $G^{s}$ that the search treats in
the same way. We use a single seed in this paper.

\subsection{Grasp pieces and the polytope graph}
\label{sec:method:graph}
For every region with $F_k\cap G\ne\emptyset$ we form the \emph{grasp piece}
$G_k=G\cap F_k$. Grasp pieces are the switch sets of the manipulation graph,
and the only sets in which the object moves. Two LPs give the extreme wrist
values $w_k^{\pm}=\max/\min\{\pm w: x\in G_k\}$ and points
$x_k^{\pm}\in G_k$ attaining them. A stroke inside $G_k$ turns the object by
at most $L_k=w_k^+-w_k^-$, and we discard pieces with $L_k<\Lmin$. Since $G$ is
thin in every direction but the wrist, a piece is essentially a wrist
interval, and by convexity the segment $[x_k^-,x_k^+]$ lies in $G_k$.

The graph has two kinds of edges. A \emph{transit} edge joins $F_i$ and $F_j$
if they intersect. Its door point is the Chebyshev centre of $F_i\cap F_j$. A
\emph{switch} edge joins $F_i$ and $G_k$ if they intersect. For it, we store
the wrist range $[a_{ik}^-,a_{ik}^+]$ of $F_i\cap G_k$ and the points attaining
its ends. Every quantity is an LP feasibility check, a Chebyshev centre or an
extreme point. No binaries and no vertex enumeration are needed, and the
number of LPs is $O(J^2+JK)$ for $J$ regions and $K$ pieces.

\subsection{Reachability A*}
\label{sec:method:search}
\paragraph{Nodes} Following CASSR~\cite{wang2026cassr}, a node does not
represent a configuration but a set reachable from the start. A \emph{transit
node} $(F_i,[\alo,\ahi])$ states that every $x\in F_i$ is reachable together
with every $\alpha\in[\alo,\ahi]$. Since $F_i$ is convex and $\alpha$ is
frozen during transit, any point of $F_i$ can be reached from any other along
a straight line. A \emph{grasp node} $(G_k,[\alo,\ahi],\phi,[a^-,a^+])$
additionally records the wrist range $[a^-,a^+]$ through which the piece was
entered and a phase $\phi$. In phase \emph{entered}, the robot is in the piece
and has not grasped yet. In phase \emph{holding}, it has just completed a
stroke. In phase \emph{released}, it has completed a return and the wrist is
back at $w_k^-$. All path information is carried by the interval. Reaching
$F_3$ before any grasp and after a stroke of 2\,rad gives the same polytope
with intervals $\{3\}$ and $[1,3]$, two different nodes.

\paragraph{Moves} Table~\ref{tab:moves} lists the successors of a node. Only
strokes widen the interval: a stroke can turn the object by any amount up to
its cap, so it lowers $\alo$ and keeps $\ahi$. Since grasping happens only
inside grasp pieces, these are the only sets where reachability grows in
$\alpha$, which is the reachability counterpart of the switch sets of the
manipulation graph. Leaving a piece towards $F_j$ can also force turning.
Inside $G_k$ the wrist moves forward only while grasped, so reaching the exit
range from the entry range turns the object by at least
$d=\max(0,a_{jk}^- - a^+)$, which lowers $\ahi$. We clip $\alo$ at
$\max(\agoal,\alpha_{\min})$. Each grasp, return and release costs 1, and
each change of set costs $\mu\ll1$ ($\mu=10^{-3}$), so the search minimises
grasp actions first and set changes second. A release must cost 1, otherwise
leaving and re-entering a piece would be a free substitute for a return.

\paragraph{Heuristic, goal and dominance} With $\Lmax=\max_k L_k$, the
heuristic
\begin{equation}
  h(\nu)=2\Big\lceil\frac{\alo-\agoal}{\Lmax}\Big\rceil+\mathbf{1}[\phi=\text{holding}]
  \label{eq:heuristic}
\end{equation}
is admissible. Each remaining stroke turns the object by at most $\Lmax$ and
must be followed by an ungrasp, either a return or the final release, and a
holding node must still release. It is also consistent, since no move lowers
$h$ by more than its cost. A transit node is a goal if $\xgoal\in F_i$ and
$\agoal\in[\alo,\ahi]$. A node is \emph{dominated}, and skipped, if an expanded
node with the same key has an interval that contains its interval at no
higher cost. The key is the set for transit nodes, and the set, phase and
entry range for grasp nodes. Because the abstraction describes reachability
completely by key and interval, this check is exact. By contrast, the
domination checks of GCS*~\cite{chia2024gcsstar} must in general be
approximated, and CASSR uses a non-admissible
heuristic~\cite{wang2026cassr}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:astar} summarises the
search.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Reachability A*}
\label{alg:astar}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Input graph of Sec.~\ref{sec:method:graph}; $(\xinit,\ainit)$, $(\xgoal,\agoal)$
\Output node sequence with grasp actions, or \textsc{none}
\State $\mathcal{O}\gets\{(F_i,[\ainit,\ainit]) : \xinit\in F_i\}$;\ \ $\mathcal{E}\gets\emptyset$
\While{$\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset$}
  \State $\nu\gets\arg\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}} g(\nu)+h(\nu)$; remove $\nu$ from $\mathcal{O}$
  \If{$\nu$ dominated by $\mathcal{E}$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State $\mathcal{E}\gets\mathcal{E}\cup\{\nu\}$
  \If{$\nu=(F_i,[\alo,\ahi])$, $\xgoal\in F_i$, $\agoal\in[\alo,\ahi]$}
    \State \Return path to $\nu$
  \EndIf
  \For{each successor $\nu'$ of $\nu$ (Table~\ref{tab:moves})}
    \If{$\nu'$ not dominated by $\mathcal{E}$} add $\nu'$ to $\mathcal{O}$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndWhile
\State \Return \textsc{none}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Placing configurations}
\label{sec:method:placement}
The search fixes the sets and the grasp actions, for example
\emph{enter, stroke, return, stroke, release}. As in CASSR, a second stage
places the configurations. We do this without a solver. First, a backward pass
from $\agoal$ allocates the turning to each grasp visit. Visit $v$ turns
$T_v=\min(\ahi_v,\alpha_v^{\mathrm{after}}+\sum_s c_s)-\alpha_v^{\mathrm{after}}$,
where $\ahi_v$ is the upper end of the interval before the visit and $c_s$
are the caps of its strokes. $T_v$ is split among the strokes by
water-filling. The pass ends exactly at $\ainit$, because the search proved
$\agoal$ reachable. Second, waypoints are placed: door points for transit, an
entry point in $F_i\cap G_k$ at the wrist value from which the first stroke
ends at the top of the piece, points on $[x_k^-,x_k^+]$ for strokes and
returns, an exit point in $G_k\cap F_j$, and finally $\qgoal$. Each waypoint
records a \emph{certifying set}, a region $F_j$ or a piece $G_k$ that contains
both ends of the straight motion into it. By convexity, it contains the whole
motion. We check containment and the coupling rule ($\Delta\alpha=-\Delta w$
on strokes, $\Delta\alpha=0$ otherwise) for every motion. Path length is not
optimised. Since the discrete structure is fixed, a convex program over the
chosen sets could shorten the path, e.g., GCS~\cite{marcucci2023motion}
restricted to these sets, or a QP as in CASSR. \todo{implement, or keep as
future work.}

\subsection{Properties}
\label{sec:method:properties}
\begin{proposition}[informal]
\label{prop:guarantees}
Relative to the regions $\{F_j\}$ and the pieces $\{G_k\}$:
(i) every motion returned is a straight line inside one region or piece that
satisfies the coupling of Assumption~\ref{ass:task}, so the path is
collision-free whenever the regions are;
(ii) the search terminates, and if a plan exists whose transit motions lie in
the regions and whose grasp motions lie in the pieces, it returns one with
the minimum number of grasp actions, ties broken by the number of set changes;
(iii) placement succeeds on every sequence the search returns.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Sketch.} (i) follows from the certifying sets and convexity.
(ii) Each stroke lowers $\alo$ by at least $\Lmin$ until it reaches $\agoal$,
so finitely many intervals arise per key and the search terminates. With a
consistent heuristic and exact dominance, A* expands a goal node of minimum
cost first~\cite{hart1968astar}. Entering at the highest and leaving at the
lowest wrist value of each switch set minimises forced turning, so restricting
entries and exits to these points loses no plan. (iii) The backward pass
mirrors the interval updates of Table~\ref{tab:moves}.
\tocheck{write the full proof; (iii) is asserted in our implementation but
not yet proven. A merged chain of overlapping pieces needs chain-aware
placement, so each piece is its own segment for now.}
\hfill$\square$

The guarantees are relative: a plan the search reports as optimal uses the
fewest grasps \emph{that the decomposition allows}. Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}
shows that this number can differ from the true one. Path length is not
optimal, which is also a stated limitation of CASSR~\cite{wang2026cassr}.

\subsection{Why not a mixed-integer program?}
\label{sec:method:miqp}
The same problem can be posed as an MIQP over keyframes
$x_0=\qinit,x_1,\dots,x_{2\ell+1}=\qgoal$ for a fixed number of grasps $\ell$,
minimising $\sum_i\|x_{i+1}-x_i\|^2$ subject to the linear transit and
transfer constraints. Region membership is enforced with big-M constraints
$A_jx_i\le b_j+M(1-z_{ij})$, $\sum_j z_{ij}\ge1$, and binaries for the
connected component of each pair. The MIQP is solved for
$\ell=1,2,\dots$ until feasible~\cite{lopezgomez2025minf}. This worked for 3-
and 4-DOF tasks, but it is fragile at 10-DOF for three reasons. (a) Big-M:
with $M=10^6$ and MOSEK's default integrality tolerance of
$10^{-5}$~\cite{mosek}, a binary accepted as 1 may relax each row of its region
by $M\cdot10^{-5}=10$\,rad. That is more than any joint range, while grasp
polytopes are $2\epsilon=2\times10^{-3}$\,rad thick. Tight per-row constants or
convex-hull formulations~\cite{vielma2015mixed} mitigate this at the cost of
a larger model. (b) Components: connected components of the switch sets were
merged through convex hulls of their vertices, which requires vertex
enumeration of thin 10-D polytopes (Qhull fails without joggling). The hull of
a union is also not contained in the union. (c) Scaling: the binaries grow as
$(2\ell+2)(J+C)$ for $C$ components, and $\ell$ is found by an outer loop. The
reachability A* has none of these issues. It solves LPs offline and does
interval arithmetic online, and the number of grasps is an output.
\tocheck{record the MOSEK status/log for the 10-DOF MIQP failure.}
